Before you change any settings, take half an hour to work out what you need to protect, and from whom. The answers decide which guides matter for you, and which steps could make your situation worse. This guide follows the method used by the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) and other digital security organisations. Write your answers on paper or somewhere only you can reach.

## List what you want to protect {#what-to-protect .essential}

Start with what you value. Usually it is information: emails, contacts, messages, documents, your location. Your phone and computer count too. EFF says that trying to protect all your data from everything, all the time, is "impractical and exhausting". The list helps you choose.

1. Write down what you keep: accounts, devices, chats, contacts, documents, photos, where you live and go, and your bank accounts.
2. For each one, note where it is kept, who has access to it, and what stops others getting in.
3. Ask what would happen to you, your family and, if you are a journalist, your sources, if it were exposed, lost or changed.
4. Add how you communicate: who you talk to, through which apps, and what you share.

The list protects nothing by itself, and it can become a target. If it names people or places, keep it somewhere safe or destroy it when you have finished.

## Work out who you need protection from {#who-from .essential}

A person or organisation that could harm what you protect is called an adversary. EFF's examples include your boss, the police, a former partner, a business competitor, a government, or a hacker on a public network. It can also be someone you trust who is careless. Their skills vary widely: some use advanced spyware, others simply screenshot your private messages.

1. Write down who might want your information, and what they would do with it.
2. For each, note what they can actually do. Someone who has held your phone or shares your mobile plan can see a lot without any technical skill. Your mobile operator has your call records.
3. Remember that attackers may target a colleague or family member to reach you.
4. Even when messages are end-to-end encrypted, the company can often see who you talk to and when: see [Know what each app can read](/guides/messaging/#know-encryption).
5. What you post can be used to write convincing fake messages to you, and attackers may use cloned voices: see [Stop before you act](/guides/phishing/#pause).

Naming an adversary doesn't tell you what they are actually doing. Keep apart what might happen and what is likely to happen: that is the next item.

## Decide which threats to take seriously {#how-likely .essential}

For each threat, weigh two things: how likely it is, and how bad it would be if it happened. The threats that are both likely and serious come first.

1. For each threat, ask how likely it is and how bad the consequences would be, for you and for the people around you.
2. Write down which threats you will take seriously, and which are too rare, too harmless or too hard to fight to worry about now.
3. Check your conclusions with someone you trust. Another person may see a risk you missed or one you overrate.

Your ratings are guesses and can be wrong. Some people choose to protect against a threat whatever the odds, and that is a valid choice. Feeling safe is not the same as being safe: check that what you already do matches the threats you found.

## Weigh what protection costs {#costs .essential}

Every protection costs something: time, money, convenience or features. Some steps also draw attention, or can make things worse.

1. Note your limits: money, technical skill, time, and people who would notice if you changed things.
2. The strongest settings switch features off. Lockdown Mode on iPhone limits some apps, websites and features. Some services fall back to SMS when you recover an account, so SMS can't always be avoided.
3. Some tools, such as those that hide what you do online, are illegal in some countries, or can be seen as a sign that you are doing something wrong. Ask whether another way reaches the same goal.
4. Back up your files before you erase or reset a device, or you will lose them. If you think it has spyware, get expert help first: a reset deletes the evidence, and restoring a full backup can bring the problem back.
5. Before you report or delete abusive messages, take screenshots: reporting can remove them.

> [!warning]
> If the person you worry about is close to you, such as a partner, a family member or someone who has had your phone, they may notice sudden changes. Apple warns that changes to sharing "may be noticed by others". Abuse support services warn that a person who feels they have lost control may become more dangerous. Before you remove a tracker, change passwords or stop sharing your location, think through how they may respond, and plan for your safety. Some people use a safer device, such as a friend's phone or a library computer, for some things, and keep using the watched one as before. If you change a password on the safer device, don't sign in to that account on the watched one again, or they may see the new password.

Acting slowly and quietly lowers the risk of a reaction, but leaves their access in place for longer. Against an attacker far away who can't see your phone, changing things quickly costs nothing.

## Choose the guides that match your risks {#choose-guides .essential}

Pick the guides that answer the threats you wrote down. Start with the ones that are likely and serious.

1. Someone could get into your accounts: [protect your accounts and passwords](/guides/accounts/), [recognise fake messages and calls](/guides/phishing/) and keep [If your account was hacked](/guides/hacked/) at hand. Then [make your accounts hard to take over](/guides/strong-accounts/).
2. Someone could get hold of your phone or computer, or attack it from afar: [lock down your phone](/guides/phone/) and [your computer](/guides/computer/), then [harden your phone against targeted attacks](/guides/phone-hardening/). If a device may be taken or searched, see [if your phone or computer may be taken](/guides/device-seizure/). If you fear spyware, see [spyware aimed at you](/guides/spyware/).
3. Someone could read your messages or learn who you talk to: [messaging apps](/guides/messaging/), then [keep sensitive conversations private](/guides/sensitive-conversations/) and [use email when it's sensitive](/guides/sensitive-email/). If you work with sources or sensitive files, see [protect sources and sensitive documents](/guides/sources-documents/).
4. Someone could find out where you are: [location and photos](/guides/location-photos/). If you need to read or publish without revealing who you are, see [go online without revealing who you are](/guides/anonymity/).
5. Your posts could be used against you: [social media](/guides/social-media/). If you are harassed or your details are published, see [handle online harassment and doxxing](/guides/harassment-doxxing/).
6. You could lose your data: [backups](/guides/backups/).
7. You are going to a protest: [protect your phone and others at a protest](/guides/protest/).
8. You are travelling or crossing a border: [travel and cross borders with your devices](/guides/travel-borders/).
9. You work in a newsroom, NGO or group: [keep a small team secure](/guides/team-security/).

A guide covers common threats, not your exact situation. If a mistake could put you or others in danger, get expert help: see the next item.

## Know where to get expert help {#get-help .essential}

If you are being attacked, think your phone has spyware on it, or a mistake could put someone in danger, contact experts instead of improvising. Contact them from a device the other person hasn't had access to.

### Access Now

1. The [Digital Security Helpline](https://www.accessnow.org/help/) gives free help around the clock to activists, journalists, researchers, bloggers, human rights defenders and civil society organisations.
2. Email help@accessnow.org. They reply within two hours to set up a secure way to talk.
3. They answer in English, Russian and eight other languages, but not Georgian.
4. They check every new request and may refuse people outside civil society. They help only with digital security, remotely: not with legal matters, physical safety or relocation.

### Front Line Defenders

1. For human rights defenders at immediate risk, the [emergency line](https://www.frontlinedefenders.org/en/programme/emergency-contact) +353-1-210-0489 answers around the clock and can put you through to someone who speaks Arabic, English, French, Russian or Spanish.
2. You can also write through their secure contact form. Replies come mostly by email, so the safety of your email account matters.

### Amnesty Security Lab

1. The [Security Lab](https://securitylab.amnesty.org/contact-us/) investigates spyware and other surveillance aimed at civil society. Contact: share@amnesty.tech.
2. It doesn't offer general technical support or training.

### CPJ

1. Journalists can ask the Committee to Protect Journalists for emergency help or safety advice at emergencies@cpj.org.
2. CPJ says it can't answer everyone and gives priority to crises.

These organisations work for civil society. If the danger comes from a partner or family member, a local service for people facing abuse is usually a better first contact; this site doesn't list one yet. No service can promise a result. Access Now says it may share what you tell it with partners or tech companies when strictly necessary.

## Plan with the people around you {#others-at-risk .recommended}

Your security overlaps with other people's. A threat to you can reach them, and the other way round. In a group, everyone shares the risks of its most at-risk member.

1. Agree with the people you work with which apps to use, how long to keep messages and when to delete them: see [Turn on disappearing messages](/guides/messaging/#disappearing).
2. In a group, check that everyone knows the risks of the most at-risk member and accepts them.
3. Look up what can be found online about your family. Ask family and friends to remove your details from their posts, and help them protect their accounts: see [social media](/guides/social-media/).
4. Contacts on your phone sync to messaging apps and cloud accounts. A number you delete in one place may stay in another.
5. Decide who should see your plan. CPJ advises journalists to share theirs with an editor or someone they trust; a plan that names people is also a risk if it leaks.

An agreement only works if everyone keeps it. Someone you trust can still make a mistake.

## Review your plan {#review-plan .recommended}

Your plan changes as your situation changes.

1. Put a date in a calendar only you can see, with a title that gives nothing away, to look at the plan again.
2. Review it sooner when something changes: a new story or job, travel, an election or unrest, a direct threat, or colleagues reporting that they were targeted.
3. If you are a journalist, do a short assessment before each assignment.
4. If someone is harassing or watching you, keep a record of what happens and when, on paper or on a device they can't reach. It helps you see whether things are getting worse.

A review on a fixed date won't catch a sudden change, so watch for these signs between reviews.
